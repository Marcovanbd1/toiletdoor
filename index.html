What Support Playlists are

Every day, millions of people leave a clinical encounter with the same unanswered question. The appointment is over. The clinician has done their job. And then what?

Support Playlists answer that question. Someone says what is going on — in their own words, however it comes out — and receives a small, curated, sequenced set of support. Not a directory. Not a leaflet. A playlist. Built for where they are right now.

A library says: everything is here, go find it. A playlist says: I heard you. Here is what you need, in this order, starting now.

The intelligence layer is Emotional Architecture — a design discipline that reads not just what someone needs, but how much they can take in, how heavy it feels, what needs to come first.

Platform agnostic — by design

Support Playlists do not belong to any system. They are a pattern, a protocol, a layer of intelligence that can sit inside any platform that captures human need.

Primary care has a home. Secondary care has a home. The public — the person who has not yet reached a clinician, or who never will — has nothing. Until now.

Help.

A Help. disc. Small, round, warm cream. A QR code. Stuck to a pub toilet door by someone who thought it might help someone.

It has always been there. You just noticed it today.

No campaign launched it. No NHS trust commissioned it. Someone picked one up, put it somewhere they thought it was needed, and moved on. That is the distribution model. That is the cultural logic.

Help. is not advanced technology pretending to be simple. It is profound philosophical thinking that arrives at something so elegant it feels like magic. A thousand engineers could not have designed it. It took an applied philosopher, inside the NHS, watching what actually happens to people in the worst moments of their lives.

One shot. That constraint is not a technical limitation. It is a philosophical position. You do not interrogate someone in crisis. You receive what they give you, and you give something back.

No login. No account. No data stored. No back and forth. No chatbot. Just: say what is going on, get a playlist.

Why this could only come from here

Mark Winterburn is Digital and Transformation Lead at Weymouth and Portland PCN — 74,000 patients, five GP practices, the Dorset coast. He is also an applied philosopher, a former prison officer at HMP Portland, a yoga teacher who trained in Mysore, a Playout Director who navigated the analogue to digital transition at the BBC.

He joined the NHS in 2024 and built Emotional Architecture as a design discipline from the inside — watching what happens in the car park after a GP appointment, on the ward at discharge, at two in the morning when someone picks up their phone and does not know who to call.

Help. is the product of that provenance. It could not have been designed from outside the system. It could not have been designed without the philosophy. The simplicity is the result of the depth, not the absence of it.
